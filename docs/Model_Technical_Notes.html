1What the model predicts

Most retention tools predict who might leave (churn). Nudgely predicts something different: how much a message changes a customer's chance of coming back. That difference is called uplift:

uplift = P(comes back | messaged) − P(comes back | not messaged)

Churn risk and uplift disagree exactly where money is wasted. A loyal regular has a high chance of returning either way, so a message changes nothing. A customer who is gone for good has a low chance either way. Only uplift separates both of those from the customers a message actually moves. Real scores for three demo customers:

CustomerNo messageMessagedUpliftTypeDecision
Aisha28.3%39.5%+11.2 ptsPersuadableMessage her
Hafiz34.1%33.0%−1.1 ptsSure ThingComing anyway: a discount is wasted
Ravi1.8%2.6%+0.7 ptsLost CauseNothing brings him back

The three types come from two cut-offs. Persuadable is the top 30% by uplift. Of the rest, Sure Thing is the top 30% by chance of coming back with no message. Everyone else is a Lost Cause. On the Lenta test set the cut-offs were +0.94 points of uplift and a 10.7% chance of returning anyway. For a real shop they are recalculated inside that shop, so the labels always rank its customers against each other.

2The data: Lenta

Lenta is a large Russian supermarket chain. It ran a real experiment and published the results for uplift-modelling research. We download it with the scikit-uplift library (notebooks/fetch_lenta.py, about 145 MB).

Why the coin flip matters. Because who got the SMS was random, the two groups are alike in every other way. Any difference in who came back was caused by the SMS itself, which is the only thing that lets a model learn the effect of a message rather than which kinds of customers happened to be texted.

Fairness. Gender, age and children are removed before training. Targeting uses behaviour only.

3Method: two models, then subtract

A single customer is never both messaged and not messaged, so their uplift can't be observed directly. The two-model method (also called a T-learner) estimates both halves separately:

  1. Model A learns, from customers who got the SMS only, the chance of coming back.
  2. Model B learns the same from customers who got nothing.
  3. For any customer, ask both and subtract: A − B = uplift.
SettingValue
Libraryscikit-uplift TwoModels(method="vanilla")
Each of the two modelsscikit-learn HistGradientBoostingClassifier: up to 300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves per tree, early stopping on a 10% validation slice
Training dataA 50% random sample of Lenta (343,514 customers), split 70% train / 30% test, stratified so each part has the same mix of SMS and came-back
Test set103,055 customers the model never saw
Missing valuesLeft blank. Gradient boosting handles gaps itself, and filling them with 0 would mean something false (for example "perfectly regular" for a customer who simply didn't visit)

Why gradient boosting, not deep learning. It is the standard winner on small tabular data like receipts, trains in seconds, and needs no tuning to be sensible. Neural uplift models (DragonNet, TARNet) need far more outcome data than a small shop produces.

The story in one line: Model 1 was better on paper but useless to a real shop, because no shop has 190 columns about its customers. Model 2 uses only what a till records, and lost no accuracy. Its higher average (57% vs 44.8%) is within the noise, so we say "cutting to ten inputs cost nothing", not "the small model wins". On a single split Model 1 scored 52.9% (90% bootstrap interval 31–89%); that is a different measurement and is never quoted beside 57%.

Each is calculated per customer from their purchases, as of the last date in the sales file (nudgely/scoring.py). "15d", "1m" and "3m" are the last 15 days, 30 days and 91 days.

Anything needing two visits in a window is left blank when there's only one. Lenta has the same gaps (77,000 customers have no 15-day variation), so the model learned on data shaped this way. The saved Model 2 is the one trained on the seventh split.

5How good is it

The main measure: share of extra visits caught by the top 30%

Rank the test customers by predicted uplift and take the top 30%. In that group, compare the return rate of those who got the SMS with those who didn't, and multiply by the group's size: that's how many extra visits the SMS caused there. Divide by the extra visits it caused across everyone. Picking 30% at random catches 30% by definition; a useful model catches more.

Why the range is so wide. The SMS moved visits by only 0.75 of a point, so the signal is small and the test set's handful of extra visits swings a lot from one split to the next. One split alone moved the figure by 30 points, which is why every headline number is an average over seven, always quoted with its range. The Qini AUC (the area between the model's cumulative gain curve and random) agrees: 0.0106 for Model 2 against 0.0069 for Model 1.

Does the ranking hold across the whole list? (Model 1, single split)

Read this honestly. The top group really does respond most (+2.3 points, three times the average), and groups 1–3 are all above average, which is what "message these first" needs. But the bottom group, predicted least movable, still showed +1.5 points. Groups 1 and 10 both hold Lenta's most frequent shoppers (26% and 20% came back with no SMS, against 10% overall), and the model sorts the movable ones at the top better than the unmovable ones at the bottom. So the ranking is trustworthy for who to message first, not for exact per-person numbers or for writing anyone off.

7Limitations: say them before a judge does

  • The evidence is overseas. A Russian supermarket, by SMS, in 2020. Not a Singapore neighbourhood shop on WhatsApp. A local pilot has to prove it.
  • No recency input. Lenta has no "days since last purchase", so the model can't use the most obvious drift signal. Its windows partly stand in for it.
  • Small, noisy signal. The SMS added 0.75 of a point. Hence the 38–85% range and the honest reading of the decile chart.
  • Labels are relative. "Persuadable" is the top 30% of this shop, not an absolute promise that a message will work.
  • Blank inputs for new shops. "Replied before" is unknown until a shop has run campaigns.
  • WhatsApp chats aren't model inputs yet. The AI tags them for writing messages, but nothing turns them into numbers the model learns from, and chats aren't yet linked to sales customers.
  • Demo customers use Model 1. The 30 fictional Kampung Fresh customers were scored before Model 2 existed.
